## Phase 2 — Team AI-On (Flat Planning, Singapore)

**Scenario:** Group flat search. Neither participant
edited context at the start — both began planning
immediately using Ask Assistant to identify best
areas. Context was eventually edited midway through
the session, not at the start.

**Constraint:** A roommate who needs to commute to
the east daily was introduced by the facilitator
mid-session. Participants did not treat it as a
strong personal constraint requiring a context
update — they incorporated it as a planning
parameter within the conversation rather than
recording it in the shared context panel. This is
consistent with the Session 1 P2 hiking constraint
bypass pattern, though the mechanism here was
instruction rather than spontaneous introduction.
A genuine user with that constraint would likely
have prompted a context update; the study framing
may have reduced the felt urgency of recording it.

**AI interaction pattern:** The AI was used for
specific simple question-answering rather than
as an open-ended assistant — participants
instructed it to find midpoints between different
locations rather than engaging it conversationally
or routing through modes deliberately.

### Val
- Noticed the other participant's messages could
  accidentally prompt the AI — cross-participant
  message-triggered AI invocation as an
  unintended interaction pattern.
- Found the ability to manually control when the
  AI responds useful. [Researcher note: auto-
  routing largely did not fire in practice,
  making manual control the effective default
  rather than a chosen mode — the AI acted as
  gated rather than ambient.]
- Research tool was somewhat useful even without
  internet access for certain queries, but
  midpoint calculation requests were not well
  served.
- Noticed the AI incorporated context from
  previous messages — specifically, a private
  residence idea from an earlier message was
  picked up in a later response. Attributed this
  to the Ask Assistant prompt having the most
  complete and structured prompt architecture.

### Jen
- Forgets to use the AI invocation button —
  expressed a preference for the AI to read
  context passively and generate suggestions
  on the side without explicit invocation.
- Stated this preference is task-dependent:
  when she has already ideated or has a
  pre-conceived direction, she only needs AI
  for further analysis rather than open-ended
  generation. This is the most nuanced
  articulation of context-dependent AI
  engagement in the study.

## Phase 3 — Team AI-Light (Trip Planning, Hiking
Constraint)

**Constraint:** A friend who does not like hiking —
introduced during the session.

**Context:** Edit context not used in this phase —
the relevance of the context panel was less obvious
in the AI-light condition. Ask Assistant used
naturally and frequently without prompting.

**AI behaviour — hiking constraint:** The AI
incorporated both hiking and shopping into its
suggested itinerary rather than planning separate
itineraries for the friend who does not want to
hike. Participants had intended to plan divergent
itineraries; the AI defaulted to a compromise
framing. This may reflect the teammate dynamics
baked into the prompt structure — the AI was
oriented toward group consensus rather than
accommodating genuine preference divergence. The
constraint was present in context but interpreted
as a balancing factor rather than a hard exclusion.

### Val
- Found the AI's compromise framing somewhat
  different from what the group intended — the
  mental model she formed after the session
  differed from what the AI produced. Noted this
  as interesting behaviour rather than a failure.

### Jen
- Compared AI-on and AI-light conditions and
  concluded the modes would have been more useful
  if the scenarios were swapped — Research mode
  better suited to the research-heavy trip
  planning task; AI-light better suited to the
  flat planning task where quick conversational
  exchange was sufficient.
- General observation: participants and users
  broadly have a strong bias toward "research"
  as the expected primary function of AI — the
  idea of research-capable AI is a dominant
  mental model that shapes expectations regardless
  of task type.

## Analytical Flags — Mapped to AQ Framework

**AQ1 — AI Routing Behaviour**
Auto-classification was largely non-functional
under real user prompting patterns in Session 4 —
a researcher observation confirmed by the absence
of auto-routed messages activating in practice.
This paradoxically clarified mode distinction for
Val: when forced into manual mode selection, the
difference between Ask and Research became more
legible through use. Jen's rough idea score
despite exposure to both modes suggests manual
mode operation alone is not sufficient for all
participants to develop routing confidence. The
fixed insight structure finding is the most
nuanced AQ1 signal in Session 4: Research mode's
rigid output format was useful for specific
structured tasks (risks, open questions) but
became a liability as task complexity increased —
suggesting appropriate routing is not just about
choosing the right mode but about knowing when
the mode's output structure serves or limits the
task.

**AQ2 — Traceability and Trust**
Both participants found content by non-UI means —
Val by chat scroll, Jen by insights panel scroll.
Jen is the only participant across the study to
use the insights panel as a scroll-based
traceability strategy. Both post-session verbal
responses directly name source uncertainty as a
concern, while both gave easy or difficult on
the survey Q1. This is the clearest confirmation
of the Q1 framing ambiguity as a study-wide
issue: the survey question measures interface
legibility, not epistemic provenance, and the
two should be treated as distinct dimensions in
the AQ2 analysis. Trust in Research mode output
remains low across both participants — consistent
with Sessions 2 and 3 — and is now explicitly
linked to the absence of source citation in
Research mode output.

**AQ3 — Shared Understanding**
Context panel was not used at the start of the
team AI-on phase by either participant — the
latest context engagement onset in the study.
The east commute constraint was introduced by
the facilitator but treated as a conversational
planning parameter rather than a context panel
update — consistent with Session 1 P2 bypass
pattern. Val's observation that the AI
incorporated content from earlier private
messages is an AQ3 signal from the opposite
direction: the AI was picking up context from
the conversation implicitly rather than from
the explicit shared context panel, which
suggests the panel's role as the authoritative
shared context source is not clearly established
in participants' mental models.

**AQ4 — Coordination Support**
Jen's scenario swap conclusion — Research mode
better suited to trip planning, AI-light better
suited to flat planning — is the most precise
mode-to-task fit observation in the study and
directly addresses the AQ4 question of whether
the workspace supports coordination behaviour
appropriate to the task. Her moderator framing
from the warm-up, combined with her post-session
observation about passive AI context reading,
describes a coordination support model that is
ambient and facilitative rather than explicit
and invocational — fundamentally different from
how the tool currently operates. Val's cross-
participant accidental AI invocation observation
is an AQ4 finding: in a shared workspace, one
user's messages can trigger AI responses
intended for another, creating coordination
ambiguity. The AI-as-gate dynamic (auto-routing
non-functional, manual invocation required)
limited ambient coordination support to zero in
practice.

**AQ5 — Contextual Catch-Up**
No formal new joiner scenario run. Val's
interest in delegating tasks to sub-processes
through action items and reply context is
tangentially relevant — it describes a vision
of persistent task tracking that would support
catch-up, but is a future-facing design
aspiration rather than an observed behaviour.
